Learning objectives

By the end you will be able to:

1 · Why change variables at all? TRANSCRIPT

The puzzle the whole video opens with:

The puzzleYou want the area of an ellipse, or the volume under a smooth surface sitting on a round base. In x and y the integral is a nightmare — the limits are ugly, the square roots pile up. But change the coordinates and the same integral becomes almost trivial: the region turns into a rectangle and the limits become plain numbers. There is only one catch — you are notallowed to just swap the letters. Something extra has to appear inside the integral. Forget it, and the answer is wrong. That factor is the Jacobian.

1.1 Level 1 — Intuitive picture

Imagine you are packing a suitcase by drawing its floor plan on graph paper. The suitcase manufacturer made the suitcase curved — when you pick the graph-paper sheet up and lay it over the actual suitcase, squares stretch into curved patches: some bigger, some smaller, some tilted. If you wanted to cover the suitcase floor with tiles, you would need to know how much bigger each patch got. The Jacobian is the “tile size adjustment factor”for integrals.

Why does an integral care about tile size? Because an integral is a sum of (function value) × (tiny area). Change coordinates → the tiny areas change size → the sum changes → the answer changes. The Jacobian re-weights every term so the sum stays honest.

2 · The 1-D rehearsal: u-substitution TRANSCRIPT VERIFIED

The Jacobian already showed up oncein your life — disguised as the chain rule. This section exposes it.

2.1 The transcript example, step by step

Transcript example

Compute ∫ 2x·cos(x²) dx.

  1. Let u = x². Then du = 2x dx.
  2. The integral becomes ∫ cos u du = sin u + C.
  3. Back-substitute: sin(x²) + C. ✓ (Differentiate to verify: d/dx sin(x²) = 2x cos(x²). ✓)

2.2 Look closely at what happened

Key ideaWhen we replaced x by u, we did not only replace the function — we also replaced dxby du, and that replacement is not free. A small step dx on the x-line becomes a small step du on the u-line, related by the derivative: du = 2x · dx. That derivative is the stretching factor.It tells you how much a tiny piece of the x-line gets stretched when carried to the u-line.

In one dimension the extra factor is the derivative. The question this note answers:

The question What plays the role of “the derivative” in two (and three) dimensions?Answer: the Jacobian determinant.

2.3 The 1-D theorem, precisely

If gis differentiable and x = g(u), then ∫ f(g(u))·g′(u) du = ∫ f(x) dx. For definite integrals, limits change too: when x runs from a to b, u runs from g⁻¹(a) to g⁻¹(b).

Same mistake, one dimension upForgetting g′(u)in 1-D is exactly the same error as forgetting |det J|in 2-D. If you would never drop the 2xin ∫2x·cos(x²)dx, never drop the Jacobian either.
Why g′(u) du is “the” stretch factor — a 30-second derivation

Chop [a, b] into tiny intervals of width Δu. Under x = g(u), each interval maps to a piece of the x-axis of width Δx ≈ g′(u)·Δu (this is the definition of the derivative: the best linear approximation). The integral Σ f(xᵢ)Δxᵢ becomes Σ f(g(uᵢ))·g′(uᵢ)·Δuᵢ. Taking limits gives the substitution rule. The whole 2-D story is this picture with “interval width” replaced by “patch area”.

3 · What a double integral actually does TRANSCRIPT

3.1 The Riemann sum definition

Take a region R in the plane. Chop it into a grid of tiny rectangles, each of width dxand height dy, so each has area dA = dx·dy. At the center of each rectangle, measure the value of f(x, y). Multiply by the area, then add:

F-riemann
Double integral as a limit of sums
∬Rf(x,y) dA  =  limmesh→0  Σi,jf(xᵢ, yⱼ) · ΔAᵢⱼ
Two ingredientsEvery double integral is built from exactly two ingredients: (1) the function valueand (2) the area of the tiny piece. When coordinates change, bothchange. The function value is easy — just rewrite f in the new variables. The tiny piece is where the action is.

3.2 Why “∬ 1 dA = area”

Set f ≡ 1: the sum becomes Σ ΔA → area(R). So even a pure “find the area” problem is a double integral in disguise — and the very first thing the Jacobian learns to protect.

3.3 Physical meaning

  • Mass: f = density ρ(x,y) → ∬ρ dA is total mass of a lamina.
  • Volume: f = height h(x,y) → ∬h dA is volume under the surface over R.
  • Average value: (1/area(R)) ∬f dA is the mean height of f over R.
Handy factIf a transformation is linear with matrix M, every region scales by exactly |det M|. Nonlinear maps scale each tiny patch by its own local det J(u,v) — the Jacobian is the “local linear matrix” at that point. (See §15 Advanced.)

4 · Transformations — graph paper laid on a curved surface TRANSCRIPT

4.1 The setup

Introduce new variables uand v, with xand ygiven by functions of them:

F-T
Coordinate transformation
x = x(u, v),    y = y(u, v)   ⟺   T(u, v) = ( x(u,v), y(u,v) )
Every pair (u, v) is carried to exactly one point (x, y) in the plane.

Think of the uv-plane as a sheet of graph paper — a perfectly square grid. The transformation picks up that sheet and lays it onto the xy-plane. The straight grid lines bend; the perfect squares become curved little patches — some bigger, some smaller, some tilted.

4.2 What transformation does to the region R

If T maps a nice rectangle S in the uv-plane onto the complicated region R in the xy-plane, then integrating over R — however nasty its boundary — becomes integrating over a rectangle with constant limits. The geometry is outsourced to the transformation; the calculus gets easy.

StrategyChoose T so that (a) the boundary curves of R become straight lines u = const or v = const, and (b) f(x,y) becomes tractable. You are buying simple limits with one multiplication by |det J|.

5 · Derivation — the area of one curved patch TRANSCRIPT VERIFIED

This is the heart of the whole subject. We zoom into onetiny square of the uv-grid and ask: how big is its image in the xy-plane?

5.1 Follow the two sides of the tiny square

Start with a tiny square in the uv-plane with width du (along u) and height dv (along v); its area is du·dv. Follow its sides into the xy-plane:

  1. Move a little in the u-direction, keeping v fixed.The point (x, y) = (x(u,v), y(u,v)) moves along an arrow. How fast does x change as u changes? That is ∂x/∂u. And y changes at ∂y/∂u. Multiplying these rates by the tiny step du gives the first arrow a:
    Patch edge along u
    a = ( ∂x/∂u , ∂y/∂u ) · du
  2. Move a little in the v-direction, keeping u fixed.Identical logic gives the second arrow b:
    Patch edge along v
    b = ( ∂x/∂v , ∂y/∂v ) · dv
  3. The curved patch is, to very good accuracy, a parallelogrambuilt from the arrows aand b. The error is of higher order (O(du·dv·(du or dv))) and vanishes in the limit.

5.2 Area of a parallelogram built from two arrows

You already know this from vector algebra: for columns a = (a₁, a₂) and b = (b₁, b₂), the parallelogram’s area is the absolute value of the determinant [3–6]:

F2
Area of parallelogram (2-D)
Area = | det [ a  b ] | = | a₁b₂ − a₂b₁ |
equivalently |a × b| = |a₁b₂ − a₂b₁| for 2-D vectors

5.3 Assemble the patch area

Substitute a = (xu, yu)·du and b = (xv, yv)·dv into F2:

F-patch
Area of the curved patch (first-order)
dAxy  =  | xuyv − xvyu | · du dv  =  |det J| · du dv
The whole secretThe Jacobian determinant is the ratio between the area of the patch and the area of the little square it came from: |det J| = dAxy / du dv. Forget it and you are pretending every patch has the same area as its parent square — true only if the transformation stretches nothing.
Why the error vanishes in the limit (rigor sketch)

Parametrize the patch by (s, t) ∈ [0, du]×[0, dv]: r(s, t) = T(u + s, v + t). By Taylor’s theorem with remainder, r(s,t) = T(u,v) + s·Tᵤ + t·Tᵥ + O(s² + t²). The straight-parallelogram approximation errs by terms of order (du)², du·dv, (dv)² in the position, hence order du·dv·max(du,dv) in area. Dividing by du·dv and letting du, dv → 0 kills the error. The Riemann sum over patches therefore converges to ∬Sf(T(u,v))·|det J(u,v)| du dv. This is the standard proof skeleton in every rigorous text [5, 6].

Alternative view: cross product of tangent vectors (3-D-ready)

Lift the picture into ℝ³ with a zero z-coordinate: a = (xᵤ, yᵤ, 0)du, b = (xᵥ, yᵥ, 0)dv. Then a × b = (0, 0, (xᵤyᵥ − xᵥyᵤ)du dv), so |a × b| = |xᵤyᵥ − xᵥyᵤ| du dv. The z-component of the cross product isthe 2×2 determinant — this is exactly how the idea generalises to surfaces and to 3-D volume scaling.

6 · The Jacobian matrix and its determinant TRANSCRIPT VERIFIED

6.1 Definition

F3
The Jacobian matrix J of T(u, v)
J = [ ∂T/∂u  ∂T/∂v ] = ⎡ ∂x/∂u  ∂x/∂v ⎤
⎣ ∂y/∂u  ∂y/∂v ⎦
columns = images of the u- and v-directions: the tangent vectors Tuand Tv
F4
The Jacobian determinant ∂(x, y)/∂(u, v)
∂(x, y)/∂(u, v)  ≡  det J  =  ∂x/∂u·∂y/∂v − ∂x/∂v·∂y/∂u

6.2 Read it geometrically

  • Columnsof J = the two tangent arrows Tu, Tv (§5.1).
  • det J = signed area of the parallelogram they span = (stretch factor) × (cosine-free since we measure actual area) — zero iff T squashes the square onto a line (Tu ∥ Tv).
  • |det J| = the factor by which an infinitesimal areagrows. det J < 0 means the transformation flips orientation (mirror), not that area is negative.
Notation alertThe symbol ∂(x,y)/∂(u,v)is one object — the determinant of the matrix of partials — not a quotient of two differentials. It is also written J, det(∂(x,y)/∂(u,v)), or JT. All mean the same thing.

6.3 When is det J constant?

For lineartransformations (x = au + bv-type), J is constant everywhere — one global stretch factor. For nonlineartransformations (e.g. polar), J varies from point to point — each patch gets its own local factor. Polar’s J = r vanishes at the origin and grows with radius: exactly the “fan” of triangles opening up (see §8).

det J = 0 ⟹ the map flattened a tiny square onto a line. Area cannot survive that.
|det J| = “how many xy-square-units one uv-square-unit becomes”.

7 · The Change of Variables Theorem TRANSCRIPT VERIFIED

F5 · CORE THEOREM
Change of variables in a double integral
∬Rf(x, y) dA  =  ∬Sf( x(u,v), y(u,v) ) · | ∂(x, y)/∂(u, v) | du dv
IngredientWhat it does
f(x(u,v), y(u,v))Rewrite the integrand in the new coordinates — the “function value” ingredient
|∂(x,y)/∂(u,v)|Area of each curved patch ÷ area of its parent square — the “area” ingredient
SThe pre-image region in the uv-plane (usually a rectangle with constant limits)
absolute valueArea is never negative, even if T flips orientation (det J < 0)

7.1 Read it as a sum (the transcript’s own words)

“Read it as a sum. Function value times area of the patch. The function value is f in the new variables. The area of the patch is the Jacobian times du dv. If you drop the Jacobian, you are pretending every curve patch has the same area as the little square it came from — and that is only true if the transformation does not stretch anything.” TRANSCRIPT

7.2 Two fine-print notes from the transcript

Note 1 — direction mattersThe Jacobian here is the one for the map from u and v to x and y — the new variables are the inputs. Compute partials of the oldvariables with respect to the newones.
Note 2 — absolute valueWe use |det J| because area can never be negative, even if the transformation flips (mirrors) the plane. Signed det J measures oriented area; integrals of scalar functions need |det J|.

7.3 Hypotheses (when the theorem is legal) VERIFIED

  1. T is C¹ (continuous first partials) on S.
  2. T is one-to-oneon S — except possibly on a set of zero area (e.g. finitely many boundary curves). No folding the plane onto itself, or areas get double-counted.
  3. det J ≠ 0 on S — except possibly on a set of zero area (e.g. polar at the origin). On a whole region, det J = 0 means T collapses dimension and the formula is meaningless.
Classic failureIntegrating over the unit disk using θ ∈ [0, 4π]: same disk covered twice → every area counted twice → answer 2π instead of π. One-to-one fails; the theorem refuses to cooperate.

7.4 The 5-step recipe (memorise this)

  1. Identifythe region R and the trouble it causes (roots, mixed limits, trig everywhere).
  2. ChooseT so that boundary curves of R become u = const / v = const lines. Sketch both planes!
  3. Compute ∂(x,y)/∂(u,v); take absolute value.
  4. Rewritethe integrand and the limits; build S (usually a rectangle).
  5. Integrate; sanity-check units and the special case f ≡ 1 (answer = area(R)).

Transcript example — area of an ellipse

  2. Then x²/a² + y²/b² = u² + v², so the ellipse becomes the unit diskin uv.

10 · Coordinates that aren’t circles at all — the tilted square TRANSCRIPT VERIFIED

10.1 The problem

Region bounded by the four lines x + y = 0, x + y = 2, x − y = 0, x − y = 2 — a tilted square (a diamond) with vertices (0,0), (1,1), (2,0), (1,−1). In xy, describing it requires cases. In its natural coordinates it is a perfect square.

10.2 The transformation

Let u = x + y, v = x − y. Then the region is simply 0 ≤ u ≤ 2, 0 ≤ v ≤ 2 — a square of area 4 in the uv-plane. To use the theorem we need x, y as functions of u, v — add and subtract the equations:

Inverting u = x + y, v = x − y
x = (u + v)/2,    y = (u − v)/2

10.3 Compute the Jacobian

∂/∂u∂/∂v
x = (u+v)/21/21/2
y = (u−v)/21/2−1/2

det J = (1/2)(−1/2) − (1/2)(1/2) = −1/4 − 1/4 = −1/2. Absolute value: |det J| = 1/2 — every little uv-square maps to a patch with halfthe area.

Transcript example — area of the tilted square
  1. Area(R) = ∬R 1 dA = ∫₀² ∫₀² (1/2) du dv
  2. Inner: ∫₀² (1/2) du = 1  (with dv still outside) → more carefully: ∫₀²∫₀² ½ du dv = ½ · (2·2) = 2
  3. Cross-check geometrically: the diamond has horizontal diagonal 2 (from (0,0) to (2,0)) and vertical diagonal 2 (from (1,−1) to (1,1)); area of a rhombus = d₁d₂/2 = (2·2)/2 = 2 ✓
  4. Also side length √2 (e.g. (0,0)→(1,1)), so area = (√2)² = 2 ✓ — two independent confirmations.

10.4 The inverse-Jacobian relationship TRANSCRIPT VERIFIED

F8
Inverse Jacobian = reciprocal determinant
∂(u, v)/∂(x, y)  =  1 / ∂(x, y)/∂(u, v)   (when det J ≠ 0)

Here: going from xy to uv, ∂(u,v)/∂(x,y) = det [[1, 1],[1, −1]] = −2, |·| = 2. Going back, it is −1/2, |·| = 1/2. Stretch by two one way, shrink by half the other.Always reciprocal — this is the inverse function theorem in embryo (§15.1).

Which way around?The theorem wants ∂(old)/∂(new) — the Jacobian of x,y as functions ofu,v. If you only know u = u(x,y), differentiate that (easier) and reciprocate: |∂(x,y)/∂(u,v)| = 1/|∂(u,v)/∂(x,y)|. In this example, ∂(u,v)/∂(x,y) = −2 (easy) → |∂(x,y)/∂(u,v)| = 1/2. No inversion of formulas needed — just flip and reciprocate the determinant.

11 · The Gaussian integral — the magic trick everyone should see once VERIFIED

The single most famous application of the polar Jacobian. We need ∫₀^∞ e^(−x²) dx — the normal distribution’s best friend — and it has no elementary antiderivative. Polar coordinates save us.

Derivation — ∫₀^∞ e^(−x²) dx = √π / 2
  1. Call the unknown I = ∫₀^∞ e^(−x²) dx. Square it: I² = (∫₀^∞ e^(−x²) dx)(∫₀^∞ e^(−y²) dy) = ∫₀^∞∫₀^∞ e^(−(x²+y²)) dx dy.
  2. This is a double integral over the first quadrant. Switch to polar: x² + y² = r², dA = r dr dθ, first quadrant ⇒ r: 0→∞, θ: 0→π/2.
  3. I² = ∫₀^{π/2} ∫₀^∞ e^(−r²) · r dr dθ.
  4. Inner integral: let w = r², dw = 2r dr → ∫₀^∞ e^(−r²) r dr = ½ ∫₀^∞ e^(−w) dw = ½.
  5. I² = (π/2)·(½) = π/4 ⇒ I = √π / 2, and ∫−∞∞e^(−x²) dx = √π.

Note what carried the whole argument: the r in dA = r dr dθ. Without it, step 4 collapses. The Jacobian is literally inside the proof that the bell curve normalises.

Why careThis evaluates the Gaussian integral that underpins the normal distribution, the error functionerf, diffusion kernels, and every least-squares/Gaussian-noise argument in statistics and physics.

13 · The warnings — where this theorem breaks TRANSCRIPT VERIFIED

Warning 1 — one-to-one The transformation should be one-to-one on the region.If T folds the plane over itself, you count some areas twice. Example: using θ over [0, 4π] on the unit disk counts it twice; the integral of 1 gives 2π instead of π. Fix:restrict to a fundamental domain (θ ∈ [0, 2π)) or divide by the multiplicity.
Warning 2 — J = 0 Watch for points where the Jacobian is zero.There the transformation squashes a tiny square into a line (Tu ∥ Tv). Polar coordinates do this at the origin (det = r = 0). That is fine — it is a single point and has no area, so integrals don’t notice. But det J = 0 along a whole curve or region is fatal: the map collapses dimension and different patches overlap. Always identify the zero set before integrating.
Warning 3 — not onto / wrong SThe pre-image S must be exactlythe set of (u, v) mapping into R — no more (double counting), no less (missing area). Draw S explicitly whenever the boundary has more than two curves.
Warning 4 — the integrandThe most common mechanical error: transforming dA but forgetting to substitute x = x(u,v), y = y(u,v) into f. You must convert bothingredients — function value andarea.
Warning 5 — sign vs absolute valuedet J < 0 does notmean “negative area”. It means T reverses orientation (a mirror). You want |det J| for scalar integrals. Signed determinants matter in differential forms and flux/orientation contexts (§15.3).
Validity checklistBefore integrating, ask: (1) C¹? (2) One-to-one on S (up to measure-zero)? (3) Zero set of det J has area zero? (4) Did I rewrite f? (5) Does ∬1 dA reproduce area(R)? If (5) passes, you are almost surely safe.
§14

14 · Where engineers actually meet the Jacobian VERIFIED

14.1 Heat conduction in cylindrical pipes and wires

Temperature fields in a pipe T(r, θ, z) are naturally cylindrical. Heat-flux integrals ∮ k∇T·n dA and stored-energy integrals ∭ ρcpT dV use dA = r dr dθ and dV = r dr dθ dz. Drop the r and your energy balances are wrong by a factor that grows linearly with radius — the outer wall would magically store more energy per unit length.

14.2 Fluid mechanics & aerodynamics

Drag on rotating bodies, vortices, and flow around cylinders are computed in polar/cylindrical coordinates; source-sink pair flows use r-based area elements. CFD mesh generators report a “Jacobian quality” for each cell of a deformed grid — a negative or tiny det J flags inverted or nearly-degenerate cells that break the solver.

14.3 Electromagnetics

Capacitance of coaxial cables, inductance of solenoids, antenna patterns: all reduce to integrals in cylindrical/spherical coordinates whose area/volume elements carry r and ρ²sin φ. Antenna near-field integrals over spherical caps live and die by ρ² sin φ dρ dφ dθ.

14.4 Computer graphics & game engines

Textures are applied to triangles via “UV coordinates”. The map (u, v) → (x, y, z) on a surface has Jacobian whose magnitude (with unit normal) gives the texel density — games use it to keep texture stretching uniform. Normal-mapping and parallax offsetting divide by the same determinant.

14.5 Finite element analysis (FEA)

Every FEA program integrates shape functions over a reference element (a unit square/cube) and maps them to physical elements. The stiffness matrix entries are ∫ BᵀDB |det J| dξ dη dζ. Warped elements with near-zero det J give ill-conditioned matrices — “Jacobian is negative at node 4” is one of the most common FEA error messages in existence.

14.6 Probability & statistics

Joint densities transform exactly like the integrand here: if (U, V) has density fU,Vand (X, Y) = T(U, V) with inverse T⁻¹, then

F12
Transformation of probability density
fX,Y(x, y) = fU,V( u(x,y), v(x,y) ) · | ∂(u, v)/∂(x, y) |

Same formula, wearing a statistics hat: the |det J| of the inversemap. “Unit Jacobian” normalizing flows exploit exactly this to build exact-likelihood generative models (§15.4).

14.7 Differential geometry & general relativity

On curved spaces the volume element becomes √|g| du¹…duⁿ, where g = det of the metric tensor — the Jacobian of the coordinate map baked into geometry itself. Change variables on a curved manifold and the √g factor plays the Jacobian’s role. GR’s invariant volume element √−g d⁴x is the same idea with a signature twist.

15 · Advanced & research-level extensions ADVANCED

Everything here is real, published mathematics. Nothing in this section is required for exams — but it shows you what the Jacobian really is.

15.1 The Jacobian as the best linear approximation (the real definition)

A differentiable map T: ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ at a point p is, to first order, its Jacobian matrix:

First-order (differential) approximation
T(p + h) = T(p) + J(p)·h + o(‖h‖)

The Jacobian is the linear map that T looks like under a microscope. det J is then the signed volume scaling of that linear image — which is why |det J| is the exact infinitesimal volume element and not an ad hoc fix. Change of variables is “chain rule + zooming in”.

15.2 Inverse & implicit function theorems

Inverse function theorem:if J(p) is invertible (det J(p) ≠ 0), then T is locally invertible near p, and JT⁻¹(T(p)) = JT(p)⁻¹ — which is exactly why inverse Jacobians are determinant reciprocals (§10.4). Implicit function theoremis the same theorem applied to “solve F(x, y) = 0 for y(x)”, and it is what guarantees a contour is smooth where ∂F/∂y ≠ 0. Both are pillars of multivariable analysis [5, 6].

15.3 Differential forms — where the absolute value came from

Integrals of densities (mass, area, probability) use |det J| — orientation is irrelevant. Integrals of orientedobjects (flux across a surface, work) keep the sign of det J, because flipping orientation flips the sign of the answer. The unifying language is differential forms: pullback of dy∧dx picks up exactly det J. The absolute value in the theorem is the density convention, not a law of nature.

15.4 Machine learning — normalizing flows

A normalizing flowbuilds a complex probability density by pushing a simple one (e.g. standard normal z) through an invertible neural network x = f(z). The change-of-variables formula for densities is the training objective:

Flow likelihood (exact, not approximate)
log pX(x) = log pZ( z ) − log | det ∂f/∂z |,   z = f⁻¹(x)

Training maximizes this exactly — only possible because the determinant is tractable for specially-designed layers (coupling layers, triangular Jacobians, FFJORD’s trace estimator). The Jacobian is not a calculus curiosity; it is the difference between an exact density and a heuristic one. Real-time densities for images and physics (Boltzmann generators) rest on it.

15.5 The Monge–Ampère equation & optimal transport

Optimal transport asks for the cheapest rearrangement of one distribution into another; the candidate map’s Jacobian satisfies the Monge–Ampère equation det(D²u) = f, a fully nonlinear PDE. Regularity of solutions (Caffarelli’s work; the Ma–Trudinger–Wang curvature condition for general costs) is a serious research front — the Jacobian determinant is the PDE’s right-hand side.

15.6 History VERIFIED

  • Determinants: known to Leibniz (1693 letter to l’Hôpital) and systematized by the early 19th century (Cauchy’s 1812 memoir; term “determinant” popularized by Gauss).
  • Functional determinants: introduced by Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi (1804–1851); his paper “De determinantibus functionalibus” (Crelle’s Journal, 1841) is the birth of the object this note is about.
  • Change of variables in integrals: folded into rigorous integration theory in the 19th–20th centuries (Riemann sums → Lebesgue theory; differential forms in Elie Cartan’s work, early 20th century).

15.7 Open / hard problems & current research

  • Exact and scalable log-det computation: O(n³) determinant is intractable for n ≈ 10⁶; research on structured matrices, sketching and stochastic trace estimation pushes this frontier (used daily in large-scale ML and lattice QCD).
  • Optimal transport regularity: sharp conditions on cost functions guaranteeing smooth transport maps remain delicate.
  • Jacobian conditioning in scientific ML: physics-informed neural networks and neural ODEs monitor det J to avoid collapsed or exploding mappings — an active engineering-research overlap.
  • Automatic differentiation (AD)never materializes J — it computes Jᵀv / Jv products exactly. Designing AD passes that exploit sparsity of ∂(x,y)/∂(u,v)-type objects is ongoing work.

15.8 Connections to other subjects

Linear algebra (determinants, rank), real analysis (inverse/implicit function theorems, measure theory: null sets), differential geometry (metric √g, pullbacks), PDEs (Monge–Ampère, change of variables in difference quotients), probability (density transformation), statistics (Jacobian in Bayesian posteriors, e.g. for constrained parameters), numerical methods (quadrature in FEA, isoparametric mapping), physics (phase space is preserved by Hamiltonian flow: |det J| = 1 — Liouville’s theorem).

FL-1 · 1-D
U-substitution (1-D change of variables)
x = g(u) ⇒ ∫ f(g(u))·g′(u) du = ∫ f(x) dx;  for definite integrals, u: g⁻¹(a) → g⁻¹(b)
Full explanation

Every symbol:g(u) — the substitution map (x = g(u)); g′(u) — its derivative, the 1-D stretch factor (units: dx/du); du — integration variable step.

Interpretation:intervals [u, u+du] are carried to intervals [x, x+g′(u)du]; length scales by |g′(u)|.

When:any integrand containing a function and (a multiple of) its derivative; trig-of-polynomial arguments (cos(x²)·2x), exponentials of quadratics, √(ax+b).

Validity:g differentiable; for definite integrals, either map limits (preferred) or flip order if g′ < 0 — never write ∫ from larger to smaller limit without accounting for the sign.

FL-2 · GEOMETRY
Area of a parallelogram from two edge vectors
Area = |det[ a  b ]| = |a₁b₂ − a₂b₁| = |a × b|
Full explanation

Symbols: a = (a₁, a₂), b = (b₁, b₂) — the two edge vectors; det[a b] — 2×2 determinant of the matrix with columns a and b.

Interpretation: |det| is the base × height; the sign of det says whether (a, b) is counter-clockwise (+) or clockwise (−) — the orientation.

Why needed:it is the entire mechanism of FL-4/FL-5 — the patch area IS this parallelogram to first order.

FL-3 · DEFINITION
The Jacobian matrix
J = ⎡ ∂x/∂u  ∂x/∂v ⎤  ;  in general Jij = ∂(output i)/∂(input j)
         ⎣ ∂y/∂u  ∂y/∂v ⎦
Full explanation

Symbols: ∂x/∂u — rate of change of old coordinate x as new variable u moves (v fixed); likewise the other three. Units: (old units)/(new units).

Interpretation:J is the derivative of a vector function — the best linear approximation to T at a point; its columns are the tangent vectors along the u- and v-grid lines.

Why needed:det of this matrix is the area scaling. Rows vs columns: both conventions give same det; columns = tangent vectors is the geometric picture.

Micro-example:for x = r cosθ, y = r sinθ: J = [[cosθ, −r sinθ],[sinθ, r cosθ]].

Common mistakes:differentiating the wrong direction (u,v in terms of x,y when you need x,y in terms of u,v); mixing up partial w.r.t. u vs v.

FL-4 · DEFINITION
The Jacobian determinant — ∂(x, y)/∂(u, v)
∂(x, y)/∂(u, v) = xu·yv − xv·yu
Full explanation

Symbols:xu = ∂x/∂u etc.; the symbol ∂(x,y)/∂(u,v) is read “the Jacobian of x and y with respect to u and v”.

Interpretation:ratio of an infinitesimal patch area to its parent square area. Positive ⇒ orientation preserved; negative ⇒ flipped; zero ⇒ collapsed to a line.

Assumptions:T is C¹ in a neighbourhood of (u, v); pointwise object — no validity restrictions beyond differentiability.

Micro-example:x = 4u − 3v², y = u² − 6v: ∂(x,y)/∂(u,v) = (4)(−6) − (−6v)(2u) = 12uv − 24.

Micro-example: (X, Y) uniform on unit disk ⇒ fX,Y = 1/π. Then fR,Θ(r, θ) = (1/π)·|∂(x,y)/∂(r,θ)| = r/π. Check: ∫₀^{2π}∫₀¹ (r/π) dr dθ = 1 ✓.

Common mistakes:using the forward (not inverse) Jacobian; forgetting densities integrate to 1 (always sanity check!).

Related:FL-8, FL-5. Deep-dive → §14.6

FL-13 · SHOWCASE
The Gaussian integral via polar coordinates
∫₀^∞ e^(−x²) dx = √π / 2   ⟸   I² = ∫₀^{π/2}∫₀^∞ e^(−r²)·r dr dθ = π/4
Full explanation

Why it matters:normalisation of the normal distribution, error function, heat kernels, quantum harmonic oscillator — all rest on this evaluation, which rests on dA = r dr dθ.

Key step:e^(−r²)·r has an elementary antiderivative −½e^(−r²); without the Jacobian’s r, the integral is hopeless in closed form.

Common mistakes:missing the squaring trick; wrong quadrant angle range; forgetting that I > 0 so I = +√(π/4).

FL-14 · TOOLKIT

Why: 90% of exam Jacobians yield to these before any expansion. 18.2 The universal 10-step procedure (any change-of-variables problem)

  1. What is asked?Area? Volume? Integral value? Density? Say it aloud.
  2. Extract givens:region boundaries, integrand, transformation (if prescribed).
  3. Name the unknowns — usually the value of the integral, or the area.
  4. Spot the concept — which row of the table in 18.1?
  5. Choose/verify the transformation.If none given, pick by symmetry: round → polar; stretched round → pre-stretch; tilted → u = combo; hyperbola → u = product/ratio.
  6. Why this method?It makes the boundary coordinate-aligned and the integrand tractable — state that reason.
  7. Compute ∂(old)/∂(new) — if given in the hard direction, use FL-8.
  8. Rewrite everything — integrand AND limits AND dA.
  9. Integrate step by step, keeping units consistent.
  10. Interpret: compare with a limiting case (e.g. ellipse → πab as b → a; unit disk → π). Reject absurd answers (negative area!).

18.4 What changes when the problem is modified?

  • Integrand changesbut region doesn’t → only step “rewrite f” changes; J stays.
  • Region grows (e.g. disk radius R instead of 1) → only limits change; J identical; answers scale as R² (2-D) or R³ (3-D) — use as a check.
  • Transformation changes (r→ρ) → both J and region image change.
  • Assumption removed (e.g. not one-to-one): split the domain into one-to-one pieces and add the pieces’ integrals (θ ∈ [0,2π) and [2π,4π) → two copies).

WP1 · Beginner · Computation

Compute the Jacobian of x = 4u − 3v², y = u² − 6v. (adapted from Paul’s Online Math Notes practice set [2])

Four partials: ∂x/∂u = 4, ∂x/∂v = −6v, ∂y/∂u = 2u, ∂y/∂v = −6. The formula xuyv − xvyu.

det J = (4)(−6) − (−6v)(2u) = −24 + 12uv.

∂(x, y)/∂(u, v) = 12uv − 24

Self-check: at (u, v) = (2, 1): 24 − 24 = 0 — the map momentarily collapses area along that curve; on either side of it, orientation flips (det changes sign).

WP2 · Beginner · Recall

Write dA in polar coordinates and find the area of the circle of radius 5.

F6. Area = ∬ 1 dA.

dA = r dr dθ. Area = ∫₀^{2π} ∫₀⁵ r dr dθ = 2π · [r²/2]₀⁵ = 2π · 25/2 = 25π

WP3 · Beginner · Direct application

Using the stretch x = 3u, y = 5v, find the area of the ellipse x²/9 + y²/25 ≤ 1.

Ellipse → unit disk; det J = 3·5 = 15; area of unit disk = π.

x²/9 + y²/25 = u² + v², so the ellipse is the image of u² + v² ≤ 1. det J = 15 (constant diagonal). Area = ∫∫unit disk 15 du dv = 15π.

Area = 15π ≈ 47.12

Generalization: semi-axes a, b ⇒ πab (§9).

WP4 · Intermediate · Prescribed substitution

Let R be the parallelogram-like region bounded by x + y = 0, x + y = 2, x − y = 0, x − y = 2. Evaluate ∬R (x + y) dA.

u = x + y makes the integrand just “u”. Use x = (u+v)/2, y = (u−v)/2, |det J| = ½. Region = [0,2]×[0,2].
  1. u = x + y, v = x − y ⇒ region S = [0, 2] × [0, 2].
  2. x = (u+v)/2, y = (u−v)/2; det J = −½; |det J| = ½.
  3. Integrand: x + y = u. So ∬R(x+y) dA = ∫₀²∫₀² u · ½ du dv = ½ · (∫₀² du · ∫₀² u du) = ½ · 2 · 2 = 2.

Self-check with f ≡ 1: ∬1 dA = ½·4 = 2 = area of the diamond (diagonals 2 and 2, area = d₁d₂/2 = 2 ✓).

WP5 · Intermediate · Polar integrand

Evaluate ∬x²+y²≤1 (x² + y²) dA.

Integrand = r². dA = r dr dθ. Don’t forget r.

∫₀^{2π}∫₀¹ r² · r dr dθ = 2π · [r⁴/4]₀¹ = 2π · ¼ = π/2

Physical check: average value of x² + y² over the disk is (π/2)/π = 1/2 — sensible, since r² ranges over [0, 1] with more weight near the rim.

WP6 · Advanced · The Gaussian integral

Show that ∫₀^∞ e^(−x²) dx = √π/2 using a change of variables.

Square the integral, read it as a double integral over the first quadrant, switch to polar.
  1. I = ∫₀^∞ e^(−x²)dx ⇒ I² = ∫₀^∞∫₀^∞ e^(−x²−y²) dx dy.
  2. Polar: x² + y² = r², dA = r dr dθ, quadrant ⇒ θ ∈ (0, π/2).
  3. I² = ∫₀^{π/2}∫₀^∞ e^(−r²) r dr dθ = (π/2)·[−½e^(−r²)]₀^∞ = (π/2)(½) = π/4.
  4. Since I > 0, I = √π/2 (and over ℝ: √π).

This is THE canonical example where the Jacobian’s r unlocks an otherwise impossible integral — it’s inside the proof that the standard normal pdf integrates to 1.

WP7 · Advanced · Volume under a paraboloid

Find the volume under z = 9 − x² − y² above the disk x² + y² ≤ 4 (z ≥ 0).

V = ∬disk (9 − x² − y²) dA. Polar, integrand 9 − r².
  1. z ≥ 0 ⇔ x² + y² ≤ 9 — our disk r ≤ 2 lies fully under the cap ✓.
  2. V = ∫₀^{2π}∫₀² (9 − r²) r dr dθ = 2π · [ (9/2)r² − r⁴/4 ]₀² = 2π · (18 − 4) = 28π

Self-check: if the cap height were constant 9, V = 9·(π·4) = 36π; removing paraboloid mass must give < 36π; 28π ✓.

WP8 · University exam · Hyperbolic region

Region bounded by xy = 1, xy = 2, y = x, y = 2x. Find its area.

Boundaries are level curves of u = xy and v = y/x ⇒ [1,2]×[1,2] in uv. Compute ∂(u,v)/∂(x,y), take reciprocal (FL-8).
  1. u = xy, v = y/x. Region maps to S = [1,2]×[1,2].
  2. ∂(u,v)/∂(x,y) = uxvy − uyvx = y·(1/x) − x·(−y/x²) = y/x + y/x = 2(y/x) = 2v.
  3. FL-8: |∂(x,y)/∂(u,v)| = 1/(2v).
  4. Area = ∫₁²∫₁² (1/(2v)) du dv = 1 · [½ ln v]₁² = ½ ln 2 ≈ 0.347

Note how FL-8 saved us from inverting x = √(u/v), y = √(uv) — ugly and error-prone. When the transformation is given “backwards”, reciprocate the determinant.

WP9 · Reverse problem · Image of a square

Under x = u + v, y = u − v, find the image of the unit square [0,1]² and its area — two methods.

Method 1: track the four corners → parallelogram → shoelace. Method 2: |det J|.

Corners: (0,0)→(0,0); (1,0)→(1,1); (0,1)→(1,−1); (1,1)→(2,0). Image = parallelogram with vertices (0,0), (1,1), (2,0), (1,−1).

Shoelace:area = ½|0·1 + 1·0 + 2·(−1) + 1·0 − (0·1 + 1·2 + 0·1 + (−1)·0)| = ½|−2 − 2| = 2.

Jacobian:det[[1,1],[1,−1]] = −2 ⇒ |det J| = 2; area = 2 × area([0,1]²) = 2 ✓.

Area = 2 (both methods agree)

WP10 · Conceptual exam trap · “Missing the r”

A student writes ∬diskf dA = ∫₀^{2π}∫₀¹ f(r cos θ, r sin θ) dr dθ and computes the disk area as 2π. Diagnose the error; show the correct value must be π; explain why.

Use f ≡ 1. What factor is hiding in “dA”?

The student dropped |det J| = r. Their “integral” computes ∫∫ du dv over a differentmeasure that counts every patch equally — but polar patches grow like r. With f ≡ 1: correct = ∫₀^{2π}∫₀¹ r dr dθ = π, while the student got 2π — double, because the missing factor r is < 1 inside the unit disk (the measure overcounts relatively more near the centre). Unit-consistency check (π·1² matches πr² at r = 1 radius) also confirms.

Moral:always sanity-check with f ≡ 1. The area of the region is the integral’s canary.

§23

Comprehensive summary — the whole subject on one screen

A double integral is a sum of function value × tiny area. When you change coordinates from (x, y) to (u, v), the function is rewritten as f(x(u,v), y(u,v)) — easy — but every tiny rectangle in xy is the image of a rectangle in uv, bent into a curved parallelogram. That parallelogram, spanned by the tangent vectors (xᵤ, yᵤ)·du and (xᵥ, yᵥ)·dv, has area |xᵤyᵥ − xᵥyᵤ|·du·dv. That determinant — the Jacobian — is therefore the exchange rate between uv-area and xy-area, and the change-of-variables theorem multiplies by it: ∬Rf dA = ∬Sf(x(u,v), y(u,v)) · |∂(x,y)/∂(u,v)| du dv. Polar coordinates are the star instance: the map (r, θ) → (r cosθ, r sinθ) stretches by r, giving dA = r dr dθ and taming every round region (unit disk → π; Gaussians; particle physics). The same law in 3-D uses a 3×3 determinant — ρ²sin φ for spherical, r for cylindrical — and in n-D, n×n. Signs encode orientation, hence absolute value; zeros signal dimension collapse, hence the warnings (one-to-one, zero-set of measure zero); inverses reciprocate determinants. Master the five-step recipe — identify R, choose T, compute |det J|, rewrite f and limits, integrate and check f ≡ 1 — and no change-of-variables problem can surprise you.

  • One line to keep forever: “Function value times area of the patch.”
  • The Jacobian is not a trick; it is what the integral meansafter reparametrization.
  • Round → polar. Stretched round → stretch. Tilted → u = combo. Hyperbolic → product/ratio.
  • Always check with f ≡ 1 (integrals are self-auditing).

Glossary — every term, one click away

Change of variables — reparametrizing an integral by an invertible map T so the region/integrand become simpler; costs a |det J| factor.
Coordinate transformation — the map T: S → R, T(u,v) = (x(u,v), y(u,v)) carrying new coordinates to old ones.
Jacobian matrix J — matrix of all first partials of T; columns are the tangent vectors along grid lines.
Jacobian determinant — det J = ∂(x,y)/∂(u,v) = xuyv − xvyu; signed area/volume scale factor.
|det J| — absolute Jacobian; the actual (unsigned) stretch of area/volume; what integrals use.
Area element dA — infinitesimal area of a patch: dx dy in cartesian; r dr dθ in polar.
Volume element dV — infinitesimal volume: dx dy dz; ρ² sinφ dρ dφ dθ in spherical.
One-to-one (injective) — distinct (u,v) map to distinct (x,y); required so no region is covered twice.
Onto (surjective) — every point of R is hit by some (u,v) ∈ S.
Pre-image / S — the set T⁻¹(R) ⊂ uv-plane; usually the rectangle you actually integrate over.
Orientation — signed sense (CCW + / CW −) of the image grid; flipped iff det J < 0.
Parallelogram — to first order, the image of a tiny uv-square; its area is |det of edge vectors|.
Tangent vector — Tu = ∂T/∂u: velocity of the image point as u changes; a column of J.
Partial derivative ∂f/∂x — derivative w.r.t. x with all other variables held constant.
Riemann sum — Σ f(xᵢ)ΔAᵢ → integral as the mesh → 0; the definition that makes dA meaningful.
Polar coordinates — (r, θ): r ≥ 0 distance from origin, θ angle from +x axis.
Cylindrical coordinates — (r, θ, z): polar in the xy-plane plus height z.
Spherical coordinates — (ρ, φ, θ): radius ρ, polar angle φ from +z, azimuth θ.
Ellipse stretch — x = au, y = bv maps unit circle/disk to ellipse; det = ab.
Inverse function theorem — det J ≠ 0 ⟹ T is locally invertible; JT⁻¹ = JT⁻¹, so determinants reciprocate.
Measure zero — a set that can be covered by intervals of arbitrarily small total length (points, lines, finite point sets); Jacobian trouble there doesn’t affect integrals.
Differential form — antisymmetric tensor integrand (dx∧dy); pulls back automatically with det J built in; keeps orientation sign.
Fundamental domain — a region covering the space exactly once under a symmetric group (e.g. θ ∈ [0, 2π)).
Normalizing flow — ML model whose density is computed by the change-of-variables formula, log p(x) = log p(z) − log|det ∂f/∂z|.
Metric tensor g — on curved spaces, dV = √|det g| du¹…duⁿ — the Jacobian grown into geometry.
Monge–Ampère equation — det(D²u) = f; governing PDE of optimal transport; determinant is the unknown’s nonlinearity.
Flux — ∫ F·n dA; oriented — keeps the sign of det J (unlike scalar integrals).
