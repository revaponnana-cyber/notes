1The One Puzzle

Find the area of an ellipse or the volume under a bumpy surface on a round base in x and y — the limits are ugly, the square roots pile up. Change the coordinates and the same integral becomes almost trivial: the region becomes a rectangle and the limits become plain numbers.

THE CATCH You can't just swap the letters. Something extra must appear inside the integral — a stretching factor. Forget it and the answer is simply wrong. That factor is the Jacobian.
Nightmare in (x, y)Easy in (u, v)
Area of ellipse x²/a² + y²/b² ≤ 1Unit disk in (u, v), J = ab → area = πab
Integral over the unit diskRectangle 0≤r≤1, 0≤θ≤2π → dA = r dr dθ
Tilted lines x±y = constSquare [0,2]×[0,2] in (u = x+y, v = x−y), |J| = ½
Sphere volumeBox in (ρ, φ, θ) → dV = ρ² sinφ dρ dφ dθ

4Important Statements — the 10 Commandments

  1. 1An integral is Σ (function value) × (patch area). Any coordinate change rewrites both factors. proof ↑
  2. 2|det J| is the area-exchange rate between uv-area and xy-area: dAxy = |det J| du dv. proof ↑
  3. 3The columns of J are the tangent vectors along the u- and v-grid lines; det J = signed area of the parallelogram they span. proof ↑
  4. 4det J < 0 means orientation flip (mirror), never negative area — scalar integrals always use |det J|. why ↑
  5. 5∬R 1 dA = area(R) — always check your new integral on f ≡ 1 first; instant self-audit. why ↑
  6. 6∂(u,v)/∂(x,y) = 1 / ∂(x,y)/∂(u,v) — determinants reciprocate; given the “backwards” formula, differentiate it and flip. why ↑
  7. 7Polar patches fan out — the factor r IS the growth of arc length (arc = r × θ). why ↑
  8. 8Translation ⇒ det = 1 (areas unchanged); rotation ⇒ ±1; uniform scale k in n-D ⇒ kⁿ; diagonal stretch diag(a₁…aₙ) ⇒ a₁···aₙ. table ↑
  9. 9det J = 0 on a point/curve is harmless (measure zero); 0 on a whole region collapses a dimension and the theorem is inapplicable. why ↑
  10. 10The Gaussian integral ∫₀^∞ e^(−x²) dx = √π/2 is just polar + the Jacobian’s r in a trench coat. proof ↑
In the exam, if they ask “compute the area of region bounded by …” — it is ∬1 dA in disguise. Choose T so the region becomes a rectangle; then Area = |det J| × (rectangle area). You can often finish in two lines, no antiderivatives at all.

5Exam Traps — where students bleed marks

  • Dropping |det J| because “it looks messy” — you silently assumed every patch has the parent square’s area.
  • Writing ∂(x,y)/∂(u,v) when the given formulas are u(x,y), v(x,y) — or forgetting to reciprocate (FL-8!).
  • Forgetting to substitute x = x(u,v), y = y(u,v) into f — transforming dA but not the integrand.
  • θ ∈ [0, 4π] → the disk covered twice → answer exactly double what it should be.
  • dA = dr dθ in polar — missing the r; unit disk area comes out 2π ≠ π (caught by the f ≡ 1 check!).
  • Spherical: dV = ρ² dρ dφ dθ — missing sin φ (the φ-arc has radius ρ sinφ).
  • Assuming det J is constant for a nonlinear map — only linear maps earn that privilege.
  • det J < 0 panic — it’s a mirror, not a negative area. Take |det J| and carry on.
  • Trusting S without mapping every boundary curve — one missed edge = missing or double-counted area.
  • det(A + B) = det A + det B — never true; det(AB) = detA·detB always true.
Signed det J
  • orientation (CCW + / CW −)
  • used in flux & differential forms
  • negative ⇒ flipped, not “negative area”
|det J|
  • actual area/volume stretch
  • used in every scalar integral
  • always ≥ 0, zero ⇒ collapse

6THE THEORY VAULT — why it all works

Every formula card above lands here. Read top-to-bottom once; afterwards jump straight to the block you need before an exam.

6.1 · The 1-D rehearsal — the derivative was the Jacobian all along ↑ back to cards

Compute ∫ 2x·cos(x²) dx. Let u = x² — then du = 2x dx and the integral becomes ∫ cos u du = sin u + C = sin(x²) + C. Look at what actually happened:

  • We replaced the function — and also dx. That replacement is not free: du = 2x·dx carries a factor 2x.
  • That factor is the derivative — it tells how many times bigger a tiny x-interval becomes as a u-interval.
  • For definite integrals, if x goes a → b, then u goes g⁻¹(a) → g⁻¹(b) — and if g is decreasing, the limits flip.
THE ONE-LINE TRUTH In 1-D the “extra factor” is g′(u). In 2-D it is det J; in 3-D, the 3×3 determinant; in n-D, the n×n determinant. The only difference is how many directions get stretched at once.

6.2 · A double integral is a sum of “function × patch area” ↑ back to cards

Chop R into a grid of tiny rectangles (width dx, height dy, area dA = dx·dy). Sample f at the middle of each, multiply, add, take the limit:

RIEMANN SUM

∬R f(x,y) dA = limmesh→0 Σᵢⱼ f(xᵢ, yⱼ) · ΔAᵢⱼ

  • Two ingredients, always: (1) the function value, (2) the patch area. When coordinates change, both change.
  • Want a change of letters only? Impossible — the patch area itself changes, so the sum changes, so the answer changes.
  • ∬R 1 dA = area(R) — the integral “knows” area; that’s why this canary catches dropped Jacobians instantly.

6.3 · Derivation — the area of one curved patch ↑ back to cards

Take the tiny uv-square (du by dv, area du·dv) and follow its sides into xy:

  1. 1u-side: x changes at ∂x/∂u, y at ∂y/∂u ⇒ first arrow a = (xᵤ, yᵤ)·du
  2. 2v-side: x changes at ∂x/∂v, y at ∂y/∂v ⇒ second arrow b = (xᵥ, yᵥ)·dv
  3. 3The curved patch ≈ the parallelogram a + b. The error is higher order in du, dv — it dies in the limit.
  4. 4Parallelogram area = |det [ a b ]| = |xᵤyᵥ − xᵥyᵤ| du dv. Done.

6.4 · The Change of Variables theorem — the deal and its fine print ↑ back to cards

THE THEOREM

∬R f(x,y) dA = ∬S f(x(u,v), y(u,v)) · |∂(x,y)/∂(u,v)| du dv

Read it aloud: “Function value in the new variables, times the area of each patch, summed over S.”

Fine print — the theorem is legal only if:

  • T is continuously differentiable (C¹) — the partials exist and are continuous.
  • T is one-to-one on S (folding the plane counts some regions twice — e.g. θ ∈ [0, 4π]).
  • det J ≠ 0 on S, except possibly on a set of area zero (points / curves). Polar’s r = 0 at the origin passes this test.
  • ∫R f dA with det J ≡ 0 on a whole region — the map collapses area to zero; every term is destroyed; the theorem is dead.
Recipe: ① spot R → ② pick T that straightens its boundary → ③ compute |det J| → ④ rewrite f AND the limits → ⑤ integrate → sanity-check with f ≡ 1.

6.5 · Why polar pays a factor r ↑ back to cards

Matrix of partials: ∂x/∂r = cosθ, ∂x/∂θ = −r sinθ, ∂y/∂r = sinθ, ∂y/∂θ = r cosθ.

det = cosθ·r cosθ − (−r sinθ)·sinθ = r(cos²θ + sin²θ) = r

  • Arc length = r × angle. The angular side of a patch at radius r is r·dθ — not dθ.
  • Radial side = dr. So patch area = dr × r dθ = r dr dθ.
  • Near the origin patches shrink to nothing — the fan closes. Far out they grow — the fan opens. r is exactly that growth.
SANITY CHECK Area of unit disk: ∫₀^{2π}∫₀¹ r dr dθ = 2π · ½ = π ✓ — matches πr² with r = 1. Drop the r and you get 2π. The f ≡ 1 check catches it every time.

6.6 · The ellipse: πab for the price of one determinant ↑ back to cards

x = au, y = bv. Then x²/a² + y²/b² = u² + v² — the ellipse becomes the unit disk. The matrix is diagonal [a, b], so det J = ab everywhere (linear maps have constant Jacobians):

Area = ∬ellipse 1 dA = ∬disk ab du dv = ab · π = πab

No antiderivative, no roots — one number did everything. Rotations and translations have det ±1 / 1, so tilted ellipses also get πab.

6.7 · The tilted square & the inverse Jacobian ↑ back to cards

Region: x + y ∈ [0,2], x − y ∈ [0,2] — a diamond with vertices (0,0), (1,1), (2,0), (1,−1). Let u = x + y, v = x − y ⇒ the region is the square [0,2]×[0,2]. The theorem wants x, y in terms of u, v — add/subtract: x = (u+v)/2, y = (u−v)/2.

det J = (½)(−½) − (½)(½) = −½  ⇒  |det J| = ½

Area = ∬[0,2]² ½ du dv = ½ × 4 = 2 = (d₁·d₂)/2 = (2·2)/2 ✓

INVERSE JACOBIAN (F8)

∂(u,v)/∂(x,y) = det[[1,1],[1,−1]] = −2 = 1 / (−½)

Differentiate the given (easy) map, take the reciprocal of the determinant — never invert matrices element-wise.

Stretch by two one way, shrink by half the other. Negative det = the map mirrors the plane; area doesn’t care.

6.8 · ∫₀^∞ e^(−x²) dx = √π/2 — the magic trick ↑ back to cards

  1. 1I = ∫₀^∞ e^(−x²) dx has no elementary antiderivative. So square it: I² = ∫₀^∞∫₀^∞ e^(−(x²+y²)) dx dy (a quarter-plane).
  2. 2Polar: x² + y² = r², dA = r dr dθ, first quadrant ⇒ r ∈ [0, ∞), θ ∈ [0, π/2].
  3. 3I² = ∫₀^{π/2} ∫₀^∞ e^(−r²) r dr dθ = (π/2) · [−½e^(−r²)]₀^∞ = (π/2)(½) = π/4.
  4. 4I > 0 ⇒ I = √π/2, and over all ℝ the integral is √π.

Without the Jacobian’s r, step 3 has no elementary antiderivative and the world never normalizes the normal distribution.

6.9 · One dimension higher — spherical & cylindrical ↑ back to cards

Cylindrical = polar in xy (factor r), z untouched (factor 1), block-diagonal: dV = r dr dθ dz.

Spherical x = ρsinφcosθ, y = ρsinφsinθ, z = ρcosφ — expand the 3×3 determinant along the z-row:

det = ρ²cos²φ·sinφ + ρ²sin³φ = ρ²sinφ(cos²φ + sin²φ) = ρ² sinφ

THE 4-LINE DRILL Volume of sphere radius R: ∫₀^{2π}∫₀^π∫₀^R ρ² sinφ dρ dφ dθ = (R³/3)(2)(2π) = 4/3 πR³ ✓ — Archimedes agrees.

760-Second Check — be honest, then peek

Q1 · Area of the ellipse semi-axes 3 and 5 — without integrating anything?

15π ≈ 47.12. Stretch map x = 3u, y = 5v; det J = 15; ellipse = image of unit disk; area = 15 × π.

Q2 · |∂(x,y)/∂(u,v)| for u = x + y, v = x − y? And the area of 0 ≤ u ≤ 2, 0 ≤ v ≤ 2?

½ (det = −½) and area = ½ × 4 = 2 (a rhombus with diagonals 2 and 2 ✓).

Q3 · Write dV in spherical coordinates and name every term.

dV = ρ² sinφ dρ dφ dθ — ρ = distance from origin; φ = polar angle from +z axis; θ = azimuth. ρ² = two circles’ worth of growth, sinφ = the φ-arc shrinking at the poles.

Q4 · Why is θ ∈ [0, 4π] illegal for “the whole disk”?

Not one-to-one — the disk is swept twice, every area counted twice. Use θ ∈ [0, 2π).

Q5 · Your ∬1 dA answer came out negative — what did you forget?

The absolute value: |det J|, never det J, for scalar integrals. det J < 0 = orientation flip only.
